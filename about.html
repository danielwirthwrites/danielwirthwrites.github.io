Daniel Wirth grew up in Rheine, a small town in Germany, in the 1980s. He now lives in the mountains of New Mexico, in a stretch of forest that doesn’t ask questions. By day he works as a research technologist; by night he is a forest dweller and a writer, in that order, according to the dogs.

He did not set out to be a poet. Poetry arrived as the thing that did the job drugs and alcohol were never invited to do. He has been broken in half twice — both times literally, two broken spines, no wheelchair either round — and each repair happened partly on the page.

He writes sitting on the ground, anywhere, because a chair has yet to improve a line. He wakes at 4:47, out of a habit the military set and never came back for. He reads poetry, science fiction, eco-fantasy, and nonfiction; walks the woods with two dogs looking for oddities; keeps a growing collection of plush keychains for reasons that have not held up under questioning; and cooks elaborate meals as though each one were a dare. By his own account he is bad at most things and does them anyway. That is the entire method.

He once read The Library at Mount Char halfway through writing a novel, decided he could no longer stand his own prose, and rewrote the whole book to be more mysterious. That manuscript now lives in the toilet, where he left it. The poems, he keeps.

Unfiltered Thoughts of a Broken Soul (2025) holds its poems exactly as they were first written — no revisions, no second takes. Tough Men Don’t Cry (2026) is the seventy-five that survived out of four hundred. Both are for the same reader: the one who kept moving while everyone assumed they were fine.
